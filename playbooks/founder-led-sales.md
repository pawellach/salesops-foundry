# Playbook: Founder-Led Sales

## Overview

Founders doing sales don't have the luxury of a dedicated sales team, a trained process, or time to learn a 12-step methodology. This playbook is a lightweight, practical guide to selling effectively when you're also building the product, managing the company, and doing everything else. It prioritises learning over volume and focus over scale.

## When to use

- You are a founder doing your first 10–50 sales conversations
- You have no dedicated AE or SDR
- You need to validate your ICP and pricing, not just close deals

## When NOT to use

- You have a fully staffed sales team (use the role-specific playbooks instead)
- You're past initial traction and optimising a repeatable motion (hire a sales leader)

## Preparation checklist

- [ ] Know who you're talking to (even a rough ICP hypothesis is fine)
- [ ] Know what problem you solve (one sentence, specific)
- [ ] Have a way to take notes during the call
- [ ] Know your ask at the end of the call

---

## Step-by-step guide

### Principle 1: Prioritise learning over closing
Every early conversation is a research conversation. Your goal is to learn whether the problem is real, who has it, how much it matters, and whether your solution is credible. Don't pitch before you understand.

### Step 1: Define a working ICP hypothesis
Use the ICP Builder skill. Keep it simple: who are you talking to, what problem do they have, what makes them a good fit? Treat it as a hypothesis to test, not a permanent definition.

### Step 2: Have conversations, not pitches
Open every call with: "I'm trying to understand whether [problem description] is a real challenge for you. Would you be willing to share how you currently handle that?" This is a conversation starter, not a pitch.

### Step 3: Listen for 80% of the call
Ask: What's the current process? What's broken about it? How often does it hurt? What have you tried? What would a good solution look like? Take notes. Don't sell.

### Step 4: Qualify naturally
By the end of a good discovery conversation, you'll know: Is this problem real for them? Is it a priority? Do they have budget? What would it take to move forward? You don't need a formal framework — you need honest answers to these questions.

### Step 5: Make a clear, small ask
At the end of the call: "Based on what you've shared, it sounds like [summary of their problem] — would it be worth a 20-minute follow-up where I show you what we're building?" A small ask is more likely to get a yes than a big one.

### Step 6: Follow up reliably
Following up when you say you will is a surprisingly effective differentiator. Send a brief recap of what you discussed and the agreed next step. Keep it short.

## Common mistakes

1. **Pitching the product before confirming the problem.** The most common founder-sales failure mode. Ask first, pitch second.
2. **Talking to everyone.** Ten focused conversations with your ICP teach you more than fifty random ones.
3. **Avoiding the price conversation.** Name a price in the first three conversations. The reactions are data.
4. **Not taking notes.** Your memory is not reliable. Structured notes are invaluable for finding patterns.
5. **Interpreting politeness as buying intent.** "That's interesting" and "I'd love to see more" are not a yes. Qualify clearly.

## Example scenario

**Founder** of Acme Corp (fictional, building logistics data tool) books a call with Meridian Logistics COO.

Opens: "I'm working on a tool to help logistics companies reduce manual data reconciliation — is that something you deal with?" → COO: "Yes, it's a real pain, we have three people doing this manually every week."

Listens: 30 minutes of questions about current state, what's broken, what they've tried. No pitching.

Qualifies: Timeline = "would want something in Q1 if it works", Budget = "we have budget for the right tool, probably $30–50K/year", Decision = "me and our CTO".

Ask: "Could I show you a 20-minute overview of what we've built next week?" → yes.

## Success indicators

- Pattern clarity after 10 conversations (same problems, same roles, same objections = valid ICP)
- At least 3 paying customers from first 20 qualified conversations
- ICP hypothesis refined at least twice based on conversation learnings
